# What Apple’s Golden Age Can Teach Us About Building Products in the AI Era

Inspired by *Creative Selection: Inside Apple’s Design Process During the Golden Age of Steve Jobs* by Ken Kocienda.

Building products feels different in the AI era. Technology is advancing rapidly, new competitors can gain ground almost overnight, and sophisticated features that once took months to develop can now be prototyped in hours. When almost anything feels possible, it is tempting to build the most elaborate or technically impressive experience simply because we can.

Ken Kocienda’s book, *Creative Selection*, describes his 15 years at Apple building Safari, the iPhone, and the iPad, which reminded me that sound design principles are timeless. Nearly twenty years ago, Apple’s teams were wrestling with many of the same questions product teams face today. Where do we start when a technology opens up entirely new possibilities? What should we build – and leave out? And how should we organize teams to make it happen?

His stories reminded me of so many moments from my own work as a product manager — late nights refining a demo before an executive review, writing and rewriting design principles until the approach felt right, and cutting the clever feature we were certain would be a huge hit.

Three lessons in particular emerged from his stories – build quality through iteration, keep the product simple, and empower small, energized teams to get things done.

## Lesson #1: Build Quality Through Iteration

When Kocienda’s team began building the touchscreen keyboard for the top-secret iPhone, there was no precedent. They settled on a few early design principles – a QWERTY-style layout, tap-only gestures, active assistance from a dictionary, and large keys containing multiple letters. The larger keys with multiple letters – common on other cellphones at the time – were intended to fit the smaller screen and reduce typing errors.

The team built a culture around “living on” the product – using it themselves, sharing their reactions, and improving it together. As they used it day after day, they found a new problem their design hadn’t solved. It worked well when people stayed focused on what they were typing, but if they got distracted, it was easy to lose their place in the word and have to start over.

Imagine typing *aluminum*. You enter the first five letters, get distracted, and look back to see that the dictionary is suggesting *slimy*. Can you immediately name the sixth letter in *aluminum* without starting over and counting? When this happened, people often deleted the word and typed it again. Despite months of engineering and several promising breakthroughs, the keyboard still created friction.

During an iPhone leadership review, Scott Forstall – then Apple’s senior vice president of iPhone software – hit the same wall. Kocienda acknowledged the frustration, grabbed a marker, and frantically began sketching possible fixes on the whiteboard. Could they try different letter groupings? Improve predictions based on common key sequences? Improve their autocorrection dictionary?

Eventually, their design lead Greg Christie chimed in:

“Aww… come on, Ken! Can’t you just put one letter on every key?”

His exasperation exposed the assumption the team had been building around. Larger keys with multiple letters may have reduced one kind of error, but they made the overall experience more difficult.

People could now see exactly what they were typing and pick up where they left off, while autocorrection helped catch typos. The now familiar iPhone keyboard was born!

I loved this anecdote because I could picture myself in the room with Kocienda. The team did not develop product taste by debating the experience in the abstract. They built versions, lived with them, demoed them, and paid close attention to what still did not feel right.

Arriving at the right experience takes time, but teams can be intentional about how they get there – demo often, use what they build, confront frustrating friction head-on, and actively seek feedback – especially the blunt kind.

## Lesson #2: Keep the Product Simple

When Kocienda and his team began designing the iPad keyboard, the larger screen size posed a new set of design questions. Should it more closely resemble a Mac keyboard, with numbers and punctuation readily available? Or follow the iPhone’s design, with larger keys that were easier to tap?

After several rounds of positive internal reviews, Kocienda was ready to demo his solution to Steve Jobs. A zoom button let people toggle between two keyboards – one with denser keys and easier access to numbers and punctuation, and another with larger letters that were easier to tap.

In theory, the flexibility was great. His colleagues especially liked the sleek animation between the two layouts.

Jobs’s response was simple: “We only need one of these, right? Which one do you think we should use?”

Put on the spot, Kocienda trusted his judgment. After navigating both versions for days, the larger keys were simply easier to use. He thought people could adapt to the additional taps, and that the iPhone’s autocorrection system would translate well to the iPad.

The choice was swift. Jobs agreed, congratulated him, and the team moved on to the next task.

While sleek, the flexible version created complexity everywhere else. Should the keyboard remember someone’s preference? Would users want different keyboards for different apps, and should the iPad remember all of those choices? By choosing one design, the team eliminated an entire set of decisions – for both users and themselves.

I’ve found that this tension comes up often in product work, and AI only intensifies it. When several ideas have merit, preserving flexibility can feel like the most user-centered choice. But sometimes, flexibility is simply a way to avoid closing doors. Every option we preserve creates another decision for the user – and another behavior the product team must design, explain, measure, and maintain.

Our job as product builders is not to offer every reasonable option. It is to understand our users and the tradeoffs well enough to make a clear, intentional choice.

## Lesson #3: Small, Energized Teams Get It Done

Across his 15 years at Apple, Kocienda repeatedly worked on small teams tackling ambitious, highly secretive projects.

His first assignment began with just two engineers – Kocienda and Don Melton. Their task was to find out whether Apple could build its own browser for Mac OS X. They started with Mozilla, a capable but sprawling codebase. After six painstaking weeks, they got it to compile – only for it to crash when launched.

Then Richard Williamson joined and tried a different approach. He focused on the minimum needed to test another open-source engine, Konqueror’s KHTML. Within two days, he had a rough demo that actually loaded webpages and followed links – more progress than Kocienda and Melton had made in six weeks with Mozilla.

They didn’t need a finished browser to make the idea feel real. Now, they had something tangible to build on and a renewed sense of energy. That was the value of a small team and their scrappy approach – they had the freedom to change course and the ownership to make a new idea work.

Kocienda’s story feels especially relevant in the AI era. AI can help small teams research, prototype, write code, and test ideas faster. It makes me want to seek out – and help create – teams with fewer unnecessary handoffs, more end-to-end ownership, and the freedom to follow promising ideas. It was clear then, and we’re seeing it again with teams at AI-native companies like Anthropic and OpenAI – give a small team a clear purpose and the authority to act, and it can make progress that once felt out of reach.

## How I’ll Apply These Lessons

The tools have changed dramatically since Apple’s golden age twenty years ago. Teams can build faster, explore more ideas, and accomplish more with fewer people.

But greater speed and possibility make product judgment more important, not less. For me, that means keeping the experience simple. Living with the product, forming and sharing opinions, and developing taste. Giving energized teams the clarity and ownership to turn ambitious ideas into products people love to use.

When the path forward feels murky, I’ll return to the process Kocienda describes – build the next demo, live with it, and invite the honest feedback that makes the right answer easier to see.
